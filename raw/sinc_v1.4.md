# Spatiotemporal baseline modeling with implicit neural fields for continuous land disturbance monitoring

## Abstract

Continuous land-surface monitoring relies on a historical baseline that represents the expected reflectance trajectory under undisturbed conditions. Pixel-wise harmonic models fit and store parameters independently, limiting spatial sharing and causing baseline storage to grow with pixel count. We present SINC (Spatiotemporal Implicit Neural Fields for Continuous Land-Surface Monitoring), which uses a multi-resolution hash-encoded neural field to generate spatially varying harmonic coefficients and predictive uncertainty. By replacing pixel-wise coefficient tables with shared network weights, SINC stores dense harmonic-parameter fields as a compact baseline representation while retaining explicit seasonal structure and spatial continuity. SINC represented a 29.4-GB two-year image stack using a 31.3-MB deployment state. Baseline extrapolation and spatial reconstruction were evaluated in eight study regions. Disturbance detection was then examined as a downstream task using uncertainty-normalized residuals, local background contrast, spectral-angle information, and empirical-null thresholds calibrated from terminal-stable historical observations, without using target-event images or labels. Across the eight disturbance scenarios, SINC achieved a macro-averaged F1-score of 0.734, compared with 0.592 for COLD and 0.558 for S-CCD, and obtained the highest event-level F1-score in six scenarios. These results demonstrate the storage efficiency of the shared neural representation and its applicability to downstream disturbance monitoring, while the relative detection performance remained dependent on disturbance type and study region.

## Keywords

Satellite time series; Historical baseline modeling; Implicit neural field; Harmonic phenology; Predictive uncertainty; Land-surface disturbance detection

## 1. Introduction

Disturbances of the terrestrial surface, including floods, fires, landslides, urbanization, and forest harvest, alter ecosystem function and represent a major driver of land-cover change at regional and global scales (Curtis et al., 2018; Mantero et al., 2020; Mir et al., 2025; Senf and Seidl, 2021). Characterizing where and when such disturbances occur, and how their frequency and magnitude vary over time, has become a central task for satellite remote sensing. Dense optical time series, made possible by the open Landsat archive (Woodcock et al., 2008; Wulder et al., 2022) and the Sentinel-2 mission (Spoto et al., 2012), have allowed this task to be approached in increasingly continuous and quantitative terms (Claverie et al., 2018). In time-series approaches, the detection of a disturbance ultimately depends on comparing each new observation against an expectation of how the surface would have evolved had no disturbance occurred (Kennedy et al., 2010; Zhu, 2017). Here, the historical baseline refers to the expected spectral reflectance at the predicted dates in the absence of disturbances. It is normally constructed from past observations at the same location, and its accuracy largely determines what can be detected and at what latency. Subtle or low-magnitude disturbances are particularly sensitive to the form this baseline takes, and improvements in baseline construction have repeatedly translated into improvements in monitoring performance (Cardille et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2021; Tang et al., 2023).

Time-series monitoring of land-surface change has been pursued through several methodological approaches. Segmentation-based methods such as LandTrendr (Kennedy et al., 2010) and DBEST (Jamali et al., 2015) partition a series into piecewise components and identify changes from segment boundaries. Break-detection methods such as BFAST Monitor (Verbesselt et al., 2012) decompose seasonal and trend signals and flag anomalies from residual deviations. More recently, learning-based methods have been increasingly applied to satellite time series, including supervised neural classifiers trained on labeled disturbance examples (Pelletier et al., 2019; Sainte Fare Garnot et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2025) and unsupervised representations such as variational autoencoders that learn representations of normal signals and flag deviations (Yadav et al., 2024). These approaches can capture patterns beyond fixed parametric forms, although their internal representations are often less directly interpretable than explicit harmonic coefficients. Parametric harmonic-fitting methods, by contrast, represent the time series through a smooth seasonal-plus-trend model and identify departures from this model as candidate changes. Among the methods of this type, the harmonic phenology model introduced by the Continuous Change Detection and Classification algorithm (CCDC) (Zhu and Woodcock, 2014) has become particularly influential. CCDC represents seasonal vegetation dynamics through a small number of Fourier terms together with a linear trend, capturing both intra-annual cycles and inter-annual gradual change at the pixel level. This parametric form has since served as a working physical description of land-surface phenology in many subsequent studies, including Continuous Monitoring of Land Disturbance (COLD) (Zhu et al., 2020), which refined the disturbance detection criterion and the per-observation update rule, and Stochastic Continuous Change Detection (S-CCD) (Ye et al., 2021), which reformulated the parameter update as a recursive Kalman filter to support near-real-time use. Across this family of methods, the harmonic model has proven a robust and interpretable representation of normal land-surface dynamics, and it has informed several widely used long-term monitoring methods based on Landsat time series.

Progress along this line has been substantial in recent years, including recursive update schemes (Ye et al., 2021) that reduce the per-observation processing cost of maintaining a baseline, increasingly flexible learning-based representations of time series (Cong et al., 2022; Reed et al., 2023), and probabilistic extensions that provide pixel-level uncertainty (Abdar et al., 2021; Gawlikowski et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2025) alongside detection results. As Earth-observation archives continue to grow and the demand for fine-resolution, globally consistent monitoring increases, however, several properties of the historical baseline remain less than fully resolved. Per-pixel parameter tables grow linearly with the number of pixels and become unwieldy at high resolution and global extent, and because each pixel is fitted independently the spatial correlation between neighboring pixels of similar land cover is left unused, leaving baselines that can appear fragmented across visually homogeneous areas and remain sensitive to single-pixel noise. Integrating harmonic models with flexible neural representations also remains challenging because recent coordinate-based approaches often predict reflectance directly rather than retaining explicit harmonic parameters (M. Li et al., 2025). In addition, CCDC-like methods typically identify land-surface changes when the normalized change magnitude, defined as the per-pixel change magnitude relative to the average root mean squared error (RMSE), exceeds a predefined chi-square threshold. However, applying a spatially uniform threshold may be inappropriate because the distribution of change magnitudes can vary across land-cover types, seasons, sensor conditions, and atmospheric conditions, often necessitating site-specific calibration. The harmonic model itself has nevertheless remained a reliable description of normal land-surface dynamics, and the strain has fallen primarily on how its parameters are stored and shared across space, on how the model is combined with more flexible representations without losing its physical content, and on how observational uncertainty is reported.

A line of recent work in visual computing offers a different way to represent spatial fields. Rather than storing a value at each discrete location, a neural field parameterizes the field as a function from continuous coordinates to a quantity of interest, with the function realized by a neural network whose weights are shared across all locations (Sitzmann et al., 2020; Tancik et al., 2020; Xie et al., 2022). The mapping is continuous and differentiable, and its shared network parameters avoid storing an independent parameter vector at every spatial location, thereby reducing baseline-storage growth relative to pixel-wise parameter tables (Strümpler et al., 2022), and the spatial structure encoded in the function is shared across pixels. Initially developed for tasks such as novel view synthesis (Mildenhall et al., 2020) and shape modeling (Mescheder et al., 2019), neural fields have more recently been explored in Earth observation as a way of representing the satellite archive itself, including a planetary-scale queryable neural data cube that encodes Landsat and Sentinel-2 archives as a continuous spatiotemporal field (Qi et al., 2026).

To address these limitations, we propose SINC (Spatiotemporal Implicit Neural Fields for Continuous Land-Surface Monitoring). SINC recasts historical baseline construction as learning a continuous, uncertainty-aware field of physically interpretable phenological parameters. Instead of directly predicting reflectance or fitting an independent model at every pixel, SINC uses a shared implicit neural field to generate spatially varying harmonic coefficients. This shared-weight encoding avoids storing a separate parameter vector for every pixel and reduces the growth of baseline storage relative to pixel-wise coefficient tables. The representation preserves explicit seasonal structure and promotes spatial coherence. SINC also estimates location- and band-dependent residual variance, allowing new observations to be evaluated using uncertainty-normalized deviations rather than raw residual magnitudes. We evaluate the learned representation through its storage footprint, query efficiency, temporal extrapolation, and spatial reconstruction, followed by disturbance detection across eight scenarios as a downstream task.

## 2. Method

SINC models historical Sentinel-2 observations as a continuous field of harmonic coefficients and residual uncertainty. Its central assumption is that seasonal reflectance dynamics and their spatial variation can be represented by a coordinate-conditioned function even though observations are sampled at discrete pixels and dates. In contrast to independent pixel-wise fitting, the shared neural field can be queried at any spatial coordinate to obtain band-specific harmonic parameters and predictive uncertainty. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the workflow comprises three stages: historical observations are converted to normalized spatial, temporal, and spectral samples; a multi-resolution hash encoder and neural decoder generate a continuous field of harmonic parameters and uncertainty; and the learned baseline is extrapolated to a monitoring date, where standardized residuals, local background contrast, and spectral-angle constraints are combined into an anomaly score.

### 2.1. Continuous representation of spatiotemporal observations

Given a historical time series comprising $N$ images, each image has dimensions $(H,\ W,\ B)$, where $H$ and $W$ denote the image height and width, respectively, and $B$ denotes the number of spectral bands. Each image is associated with an acquisition date $T$. Rather than treating each image as an independent raster array, SINC reorganizes each valid pixel observation as a combination of its spatial coordinate $x$, normalized acquisition time $t$, and multiband surface reflectance. Accordingly, for a pixel observed at spatial location $\mathbf{x}$ and time $t$, the coordinate-conditioned mapping between the spatiotemporal coordinates and the corresponding multiband reflectance is expressed as follows:

$$
\Phi_{\theta}\left( \mathbf{x},t \right) \rightarrow \left\lbrack r_{1},r_{2},\cdots r_{B} \right\rbrack \in R^{B} \tag{1}
$$

where $\Phi_{\theta}$ denotes the coordinate-conditioned reflectance mapping learned by SINC, and $\theta$ represents the complete set of learnable model parameters. During training, each valid observation $\left( \mathbf{x},\ t,\ \left\lbrack r_{1},r_{2},\cdots r_{B} \right\rbrack \right)$ provides a sample for estimating this mapping. The term $r_{b}$ represents the surface reflectance of the $b$-th spectral band, and $R^{B}$ denotes the $B$-dimensional real-valued reflectance space. The specific parameterization of $\Phi_{\theta}$ is described in Section 2.2. This study uses seven Sentinel-2 spectral bands (B2, B3, B4, B5, B8, B11, and B12), spanning the visible, red-edge, near-infrared (NIR), and shortwave-infrared (SWIR) spectral regions (Spoto et al., 2012). The original digital numbers (DNs) are converted to surface reflectance using a scale factor of 0.0001. Reflectance values outside \[0, 1.2\] are treated as invalid, and a valid training sample is required to have reflectance greater than 0.001 in all seven bands to reduce the influence of extremely low-reflectance noise on model optimization.

The spatial and temporal coordinates used in Eq. (1) are normalized before being provided to the network. Any pixel location is represented by a continuous coordinate $\mathbf{x}\mathbf{\ } = \ (x,y)$, and its acquisition date is represented by the normalized time variable $t$. Spatial coordinates are normalized to the pixel center using Eq. (2):

$$
x = \frac{j + 0.5}{W},\ y = \frac{i + 0.5}{H} \tag{2}
$$

where $i$ and $j$ denote the spatial row and column indices, and $H$ and $W$ represent the image height and width, respectively. Normalizing the spatial coordinates to \[0,1\] provides a consistent numerical input domain for images with different spatial dimensions and ensures that each query coordinate corresponds to the physical center of a pixel.

The time variable is normalized over the training time window according to Eq. (3):

$$
t = \frac{T - T_{start}}{T_{end} - T_{start}} \tag{3}
$$

where $T$ is the image acquisition date, and $T_{start}$ and $T_{end}$ are the start and end dates of the training sequence, respectively. This operation maps historical sequences with different temporal spans to a common numerical scale, providing a consistent temporal coordinate for modeling seasonal cycles, long-term trends, and extrapolation beyond the training period.

Through this representation, the original remote-sensing image sequence is converted into a set of multiband observations indexed by continuous spatiotemporal coordinates. SINC subsequently uses the spatial coordinate $\mathbf{x}$ to query a learned continuous field of spatially varying model parameters and combines the decoded parameters with the time variable $t$ to synthesize the normal reflectance baseline for a specified location and acquisition date, as detailed in Section 2.2.

### 2.2. Implicit neural representation of harmonic baseline parameters

The purpose of SINC is to represent normal surface-reflectance trajectories through spatially varying harmonic parameters, rather than constructing an independent time-series model for each pixel. Although Sentinel-2 observations are acquired at discrete and irregular intervals, and may contain residual observational noise, normal land-surface reflectance generally exhibits seasonal variation and long-term trends. SINC therefore uses a neural network to generate the parameters of a harmonic time-series model from spatial coordinates. This design retains the temporal structure of established harmonic models (Ye et al., 2021; Zhu and Woodcock, 2014), while allowing the parameters governing these trajectories to vary continuously over space.

The implementation is illustrated in Fig. 2. The normalized spatial coordinate $\mathbf{x}$ defined in Eq. (2) is first encoded by a multi-resolution hash grid to obtain a latent spatial feature $z$. A static multilayer perceptron (MLP) then decodes $z$ into band-specific harmonic and uncertainty parameters. The harmonic parameters are combined with the normalized acquisition time$\ t$ defined in Eq. (3) to synthesize the expected reflectance baseline. The encoder and decoder described below are components of the coordinate-conditioned mapping $\Phi_{\theta}\left( \mathbf{x},\ t \right)$ introduced in Eq. (1). Fig. 2 focuses on the implicit spatial representation and harmonic baseline synthesis, whereas the dynamic state correction is introduced separately in Section 2.4.

Following the architecture shown in Fig. 2, the normalized spatial coordinate $\mathbf{x} = (x,y)$ is evaluated at $L$ hash-grid resolution levels. At each level, the trainable features stored at the neighboring grid vertices are retrieved and bilinearly interpolated at $x$. The interpolated features from all resolution levels are then concatenated:

$$
\mathbf{z} = E\left( \mathbf{x} \right) = \left\lbrack f_{1}(x),...,f_{L}(x) \right\rbrack \tag{4}
$$

where $E$ denotes the multi-resolution hash-grid encoder, $f_{l}(x)$ is the interpolated feature at the $l$-th resolution level, and $L$ is the total number of resolution levels. In the current implementation, $L = 12$. Because neighboring coordinates share partially overlapping grid vertices, their encoded features are locally related. The multi-resolution structure allows the encoder to represent both broad spatial variation and finer land-surface boundaries (Müller et al., 2022).

The latent spatial feature $z$ is subsequently passed to the static MLP decoder $F$:

$$
F(\mathbf{z}){= \left\{ \mathbf{p}_{b}(\mathbf{x}) \right\}}_{b = 1}^{B} \tag{5}
$$

where $p_{b}(\mathbf{x})$ denotes the parameter vector decoded for the $b$-th spectral band, and $B$ is the number of Sentinel-2 bands defined in Section 2.1. For each band, the decoded vector contains the trend and harmonic coefficients used in Eq. (6), together with the uncertainty-related quantities used in Section 2.3. Because the same encoder $E$ and decoder $F$ are evaluated at every spatial coordinate, the decoded parameters form a continuous spatial parameter field rather than independent pixel-wise parameter tables.

The harmonic parameters decoded using Eq. (5), together with the additive dynamic state correction introduced in Section 2.4, are used to construct the final expected reflectance baseline for the $b$-th band:

$$
\mu_{b}\left( \mathbf{x},t \right) = 0.5 + d_{b}\left( \mathbf{x},\widetilde{t} \right) + \alpha_{b}\left( \mathbf{x} \right)\widetilde{t} + \sum_{k = 1}^{K}\left\lbrack \left( c_{b,k}^{0}\left( \mathbf{x} \right) + c_{b,k}^{1}\left( \mathbf{x} \right)\widetilde{t} \right)\cos{\omega_{k}t} + \left( s_{b,k}^{0}\left( \mathbf{x} \right) + s_{b,k}^{1}\left( \mathbf{x} \right)\widetilde{t} \right)\sin{\omega_{k}t} \right\rbrack \tag{6}
$$

where $\mu_{b}\left( \mathbf{x},t \right)$ is the final expected reflectance baseline, whereas $r_{b}$ in Eq. (1) is the corresponding observed reflectance. The term $d_{b}\left( \mathbf{x},\widetilde{t} \right)$ denotes the additive dynamic state correction defined in Section 2.4. The constant 0.5 is the fixed reflectance offset used to center the modeled trajectory. The parameter $\alpha_{b}\left( \mathbf{x} \right)$ represents the long-term trend; $c_{b,k}^{0}\left( \mathbf{x} \right)$ and $s_{b,k}^{0}\left( \mathbf{x} \right)$ are the base cosine and sine coefficients of the $k$-th harmonic; and $c_{b,k}^{1}\left( \mathbf{x} \right)$ and $s_{b,k}^{1}\left( \mathbf{x} \right)$ describe gradual changes in harmonic amplitude over time. The truncated time variable is defined as $\widetilde{t} = \min(t,1)$. The harmonic frequencies are defined as:

$$
\omega_{k} = 2\pi kY \tag{7}
$$

where $Y$ is the number of years corresponding to the training time span. In the current implementation, the harmonic order $K$ is a fixed hyperparameter set to $K = 3$. SINC adaptively optimizes the harmonic coefficients across different bands and locations in a data-driven manner, rather than automatically altering the number of harmonic terms.

During temporal extrapolation, the non-periodic and periodic components in Eq. (6) are treated differently. The trend and amplitude-change terms use $\widetilde{t}$, so their values remain fixed after the end of the training period when $t > 1$. In contrast, the sine and cosine terms retain the original normalized time $t$, allowing the seasonal cycle to continue beyond the training window. This treatment prevents the non-periodic components from increasing without bound while preserving the temporal progression of the seasonal baseline. The additive dynamic state correction used to obtain the final expected reflectance is described in Section 2.4, and the estimation of predictive uncertainty is presented in Section 2.3.

### 2.3. Heteroscedastic uncertainty estimation for baseline learning

As shown in Fig. 2, for the $b$-th spectral band, SINC synthesizes the expected reflectance $\mu_{b}\left( \mathbf{x},\ t \right)$ using Eq. (6). The static decoder additionally outputs a base log-variance $l_{b}(\mathbf{x})$, a temporal variance coefficient $\beta_{b}(\mathbf{x})$, and a decay parameter $\gamma_{b}(\mathbf{x})$. The time-dependent predictive log-variance is defined as

$$
\log\sigma_{b}^{2}\left( \mathbf{x,}t \right) = l_{b}\left( \mathbf{x} \right) + \beta_{b}\left( \mathbf{x} \right)\left( \widetilde{t} - 1 \right) + 2\log{1 + \gamma_{b}(\mathbf{x}){(t - 1)}_{+}} \tag{8}
$$

In Eq. (8), $\widetilde{t} = min(\max(t,0),1)$ and ${(u)}_{+} = max(u,0)$. The first two terms describe spatially, spectrally, and temporally varying residual dispersion within the training period, whereas the final term increases the predictive variance with extrapolation distance. The predicted standard deviation is $\sigma_{b}\left( \mathbf{x},t \right) = e^{\frac{1}{2}\log\sigma_{b}^{2}(\mathbf{x,}t)}$. Together, $\mu_{b}\left( \mathbf{x},t \right)$and $\sigma_{b}\left( \mathbf{x},t \right)$ define the probabilistic reflectance baseline.

Assuming that the reflectance residuals are conditionally Gaussian, SINC estimates the predictive mean and variance using a heteroscedastic negative log-likelihood loss (Abdar et al., 2021; Gawlikowski et al., 2023; Kendall and Gal, 2017; Nix and Weigend, 1994). Ignoring the additive constant that is independent of the model parameters, the loss for one valid observation and spectral band is defined as:

$$
L_{NLL} = {\frac{1}{2}e}^{( - \log\sigma_{b}^{2}(\mathbf{x,}t))}{\lbrack r_{b} - \mu_{b}\left( \mathbf{x},t \right)\rbrack}^{2} + \frac{1}{2}\log\sigma_{b}^{2}(\mathbf{x,}t) \tag{9}
$$

where $\sigma_{b}\left( \mathbf{x,}t \right)$ is the predicted standard deviation for the $b$-th band. The first term in Eq. (9) weights the squared residual by the inverse of the predicted variance. Consequently, residuals associated with lower predicted uncertainty receive a stronger penalty, whereas residuals associated with larger predicted variances are assigned a lower contribution. The second term penalizes unnecessarily large variance estimates, preventing the model from reducing the residual term merely by inflating its predicted uncertainty. Eq. (9) is evaluated for every valid observation and spectral band. In the current implementation, $\log\sigma_{b}^{2}(\mathbf{x,}t)$ is clamped to \[-7,5\] to maintain numerical stability during optimization.

In addition to the log-variance, the static decoder learns a spatially and spectrally varying time-decay parameter $\gamma_{b}(\mathbf{x})$. This parameter is used to assign time-dependent weights to historical observations:

$$
w_{b}\left( \mathbf{x},t \right) = e^{- \frac{\gamma_{b}(\mathbf{x})(1 - t)Y}{2}} + 0.5 \tag{10}
$$

where $t$ is the normalized acquisition time defined in Eq. (3), and $Y$ is the training time span in years defined in Eq. (7). In the current implementation, $\gamma_{b}(\mathbf{x})$ is constrained to \[0.1,5.0\]. As $t$ approaches 1, $w_{b}\left( \mathbf{x},\ t \right)$ increases, giving observations closer to the end of the training period greater influence during model optimization. The additive constant 0.5 prevents early observations from receiving negligible weights and preserves their contribution to the estimation of seasonal cycles and long-term trends.

During training, the heteroscedastic loss in Eq. (9) is multiplied by the time-dependent weight in Eq. (10) before being averaged over all valid observations and spectral bands. The resulting weighted probabilistic loss is subsequently combined with the dynamic-state regularization term introduced in Section 2.4, where the complete training objective is defined.

### 2.4. Dynamic state correction of the harmonic baseline

The expected reflectance baseline in Eq. (6) contains a harmonic component and an additive dynamic correction. The harmonic component represents repeatable seasonal variation, gradual changes in seasonal amplitude, and long-term trends. However, historical reflectance trajectories may also exhibit sustained changes in their baseline level that cannot be adequately represented by the harmonic component alone. Forcing the harmonic coefficients to account for both seasonal variation and non-periodic state changes may distort the estimated seasonal trajectory. SINC therefore introduces the additive term ${\ d}_{b}\left( \mathbf{x},\widetilde{t}\  \right)$ to adjust the baseline level without altering the harmonic frequencies or coefficients.

The dynamic branch takes the latent spatial feature $\mathbf{z}$ obtained from Eq. (4) and the truncated normalized time$\ \widetilde{t}$ as inputs. To separate the dynamic correction from the static harmonic intercept, the branch is anchored at the beginning of the normalized training period. The band-specific correction vector is defined as:

$$
\mathbf{d}\left( \mathbf{x},\widetilde{t} \right) = D\left( \mathbf{z},\widetilde{t} \right) - D\left( \mathbf{z},0 \right) = \left\lbrack {d_{1}\left( \mathbf{x},\widetilde{t} \right),\ldots d}_{B}\left( \mathbf{x},\widetilde{t} \right) \right\rbrack \tag{11}
$$

where $B$ is the number of spectral bands defined in Section 2.1, and $d_{b}\left( \mathbf{x},\widetilde{t} \right)$ is the correction term included in Eq. (6) for the$\ b$-th band. Subtracting $D\left( \mathbf{z},0 \right)$ in Eq. (11) anchors the dynamic correction at zero when $t = 0$ and reduces the ambiguity between the harmonic intercept and the dynamic branch. The dynamic branch does not independently reconstruct the complete reflectance trajectory. Instead, its output is added to the harmonic component to obtain the final expected reflectance baseline$\mu_{b}\left( \mathbf{x},t \right)$.

The relationship among the harmonic component, dynamic correction, and final baseline is illustrated in Fig. 3.

To prevent the dynamic branch from fitting isolated temporal fluctuations or residual observational noise, a TV regularization term is applied to corrections evaluated at adjacent time coordinates, as defined in Eq. (12):

$$
\mathcal{L}_{TV} = \frac{1}{N_{s}B}\sum_{n = 1}^{N_{s}}{\sum_{b = 1}^{B}\left| d_{b}\left( \mathbf{x}_{n},\min\left( t_{n} + \Delta t,1 \right) \right) - d_{b}\left( \mathbf{x}_{n},{\widetilde{t}}_{n} \right) \right|} \tag{12}
$$

where $N_{s}$ is the number of valid spatiotemporal samples in a training batch, ${\widetilde{t}}_{n} = min\left( t_{n},1 \right)$, and $\Delta t = 0.01$. This regularization penalizes abrupt changes in the dynamic correction over short temporal intervals, encouraging the branch to represent smooth baseline-level adjustments rather than scene-specific noise. The timing and magnitude of the adjustment are learned directly from the historical observations and no explicit change-point time is provided to the model.

The complete training objective combines the time-weighted heteroscedastic loss in Eqs. (9) and (10) with the TV regularization term in Eq. (12), as given by Eq. (13):

$$
\mathcal{L =}\frac{1}{N_{s}B}\sum_{n = 1}^{N_{s}}{\sum_{b = 1}^{B}w_{b}}\left( \mathbf{x}_{n},t_{n} \right)\mathcal{L}_{NLL}\left( \mathbf{x}_{n},t_{n},b \right) + \delta_{TV}\mathcal{L}_{TV} + \frac{\delta_{mag}}{N_{s}B}\sum_{n = 1}^{N_{s}}{\sum_{b = 1}^{B}\left| d_{b}\left( \mathbf{x}_{n},{\widetilde{t}}_{n} \right) \right|} \tag{13}
$$

where $\delta_{TV}$ and $\delta_{mag}$ control the contributions of the TV regularization and dynamic-correction magnitude regularization, respectively, and both are set to 0.0005 in the current implementation. The first term jointly estimates the expected reflectance and its predictive uncertainty, the second term constrains the temporal variation of the dynamic correction, and the third term limits its overall magnitude to reduce overlap with the harmonic component.

During temporal extrapolation, $\widetilde{t}$ remains fixed at 1. The additive correction is therefore held at its end-of-training value and enters the extrapolated baseline as a constant offset. Because this offset was learned jointly with the harmonic coefficients, it absorbs sustained, non-periodic state changes that occurred during training and keeps the monitoring baseline at the last constrained surface state, rather than allowing unconstrained drift beyond the observed temporal range. In contrast, the original normalized time $t$ remains in the sine and cosine terms of Eq. (6), so the seasonal cycle continues from that state. Subsequent observations are then compared with a baseline that preserves the end-of-training level while retaining the learned phenological rhythm. The resulting expected reflectance $\mu_{b}\left( \mathbf{x},t \right)$ and predictive standard deviation $\sigma_{b}\left( \mathbf{x,}t \right)$ jointly define the probabilistic baseline used for anomaly detection in Section 2.5.

### 2.5. Anomaly detection from uncertainty-normalized baseline deviations

For a new Sentinel-2 observation acquired at date $T$, its normalized time $t$ is calculated using Eq. (3). SINC then queries the stored .gndc representation to obtain the expected reflectance $\mu_{b}\left( \mathbf{x},t \right)$ and predictive standard deviation $\sigma_{b}\left( \mathbf{x,}t \right)$ for each pixel and spectral band. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the deviation from this probabilistic baseline is evaluated using its multiband residual magnitude, local spatial contrast, and spectral angular difference.

The multiband squared standardized residual statistic is calculated using Eq. (14):

$$
\chi^{2}\left( \mathbf{x} \right) = \sum_{b = 1}^{B}\left( \frac{r_{b}(\mathbf{x}) - \mu_{b}\left( \mathbf{x},t \right)}{\sigma_{b}\left( \mathbf{x}\mathbf{,}t \right) + \epsilon} \right)^{2} \tag{14}
$$

where $r_{b}(\mathbf{x})$ is the observed reflectance, $B$ is the number of spectral bands, and $\epsilon = 0.005$ prevents excessive residual amplification when the predicted uncertainty is very small. Here, $\chi^{2}\left( \mathbf{x} \right)$ denotes a squared standardized-residual statistic; its threshold is estimated empirically rather than from a theoretical chi-square distribution.

Local spatial contrast is measured using multi-scale Constant False Alarm Rate (CFAR) filtering. Three donut kernels with outer/inner sizes of (11, 3), (25, 9), and (51, 21) pixels are applied to $\chi^{2}\left( \mathbf{x} \right)$. For each scale $m$, the center response is compared with the local background mean $B_{m}(\mathbf{x})$, and the maximum non-negative multi-scale CFAR response is calculated using Eq. (15):

$$
S_{CFAR}\left( \mathbf{x} \right) = {_{m}^{\max}\left\lbrack \chi^{2}\left( \mathbf{x} \right) - B_{m}\left( \mathbf{x} \right) \right\rbrack}_{+} \tag{15}
$$

where $\lbrack u\rbrack_{+} = max(u,\ 0)$. In parallel, the Spectral Angle Mapper (SAM) (Kruse et al., 1993) response is calculated using Eq. (16):

$$
S_{SAM}\left( \mathbf{x} \right) = \arccos\left( \frac{r\left( \mathbf{x} \right) \bullet \mu\left( \mathbf{x},t \right)}{\left\| r\left( \mathbf{x} \right) \right\|\left\| \mu\left( \mathbf{x},t \right) \right\| + \eta} \right) \tag{16}
$$

where $\eta = 10^{- 9}$ prevents division by zero. For numerical stability, the cosine argument was clipped to \[-1, 1\] before applying the arccosine function.

The empirical-null responses are estimated from the terminal stable state of the pre-event historical baseline period. For each pixel, changes in the learned dynamic correction between consecutive observations are normalized by the actual elapsed time. A state transition is identified when this change rate exceeds a robust threshold based on the median and scaled median absolute deviation. The terminal stable state begins after the latest detected transition and a one-observation guard interval, and must contain at least three valid observations spanning at least 30 days. This procedure reduces contamination from earlier surface-state changes while retaining the most recent stable observations available before the monitoring date.

The retained dates are divided into calibration and validation subsets. The 95th percentiles of the calibration responses define the reference scales $q_{\chi^{2}}$, $q_{CFAR}$, and $q_{SAM}$. No fixed lower bound is imposed on the SAM reference scale. The calibration procedure uses only observations preceding the target event; the target image and its disturbance labels are not used.

Finally, the three normalized responses are combined into the joint score defined in Eq. (17):

$$
g\left( \mathbf{x} \right) = \min\left\lbrack \frac{\chi^{2}\left( \mathbf{x} \right)}{q_{\chi^{2}}},\max\left( \frac{S_{CFAR}\left( \mathbf{x} \right)}{q_{CFAR}},\frac{S_{SAM}\left( \mathbf{x} \right)}{q_{SAM}} \right) \right\rbrack \tag{17}
$$

The empirical-null threshold $\tau_{g}$ is defined as the $1 - \alpha$ quantile of the joint scores obtained from calibration observations retained from the terminal stable historical state, with $\alpha = 0.01$ in the current implementation (Efron, 2004; Ruff et al., 2021). For interpretation in the original response domains, the corresponding effective thresholds are$\tau_{\chi^{2}} = \tau_{g}q_{\chi^{2}}$, $\tau_{CFAR} = \tau_{g}q_{CFAR}$, and $\tau_{SAM} = \tau_{g}q_{SAM}$. A pixel is classified as anomalous when$\ g\left( \mathbf{x} \right) > \tau_{g}$. This decision requires both the normalized multiband residual response and at least one of the normalized CFAR or SAM responses to exceed the common empirical-null decision level $\tau_{g}$. All calibration parameters are fixed before disturbance evaluation, and the resulting raw binary mask is used for accuracy assessment without morphological post-processing.

## 3. Materials and experimental setup

### 3.1. Study area selection and representative scenarios

Eight study regions were selected to cover different disturbance mechanisms and environmental settings (Table 1 and Fig. 5): flooding at Miyun Reservoir in Beijing (Yao et al., 2026); an earthquake-monitoring region near the Pazarcık segment of the East Anatolian Fault in southern Türkiye (Güvercin et al., 2022), where a local earthquake was recorded on 29 January 2021 (AFAD, 2021),a landslide in Enga Province, Papua New Guinea (Z. Li et al., 2025); Hurricane Milton in the Gulf of Mexico (Hagen, 2025); wildfires near Greenville and Los Angeles, California (Galodha and Sharma, 2025; Garner and Kovacik, 2023); and deforestation in Jiangxi, China (Liang et al., 2025), and Kakinada, Andhra Pradesh, India (Jayakumar, 2023). A separate SINC baseline was trained for each region. The experiments therefore evaluate within-region temporal monitoring across different disturbance types rather than the transfer of a single trained model between regions.

### 3.2. Data acquisition and preprocessing

For these regions, long-term Sentinel-2 surface reflectance data were acquired via the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform (Gorelick et al., 2017). To maximize the extraction of valid observations in cloud-prone disaster sites, we utilized the Cloud Score Plus (CS+) quality assessment algorithm with a cumulative distribution function (CDF) threshold of 0.6 to achieve pixel-level masking of clouds, cloud shadows, and atmospheric aerosols (Pasquarella et al., 2023). Given that the SINC framework is built upon implicit neural fields, which possess inherent advantages in handling spatiotemporal data gaps, we implemented a valid pixel ratio filtering strategy: an image was included in the training sequence only if the proportion of clear pixels exceeded 40%. Ultimately, seven core bands (B2, B3, B4, B5, B8, B11, B12) including visible, red-edge, and shortwave-infrared (SWIR) spectra were extracted and spatially resampled to 20 m using bilinear interpolation, providing standardized multidimensional spectral time-series inputs for the model.

### 3.3. Experimental design

The evaluation addressed three questions. First, baseline extrapolation and spatial reconstruction were examined in chronological pre-event holdout periods without mapped target disturbances by comparison with the harmonic baselines used by COLD and S-CCD. Second, disturbance detection was evaluated across eight event scenarios. Third, ablation experiments examined the roles of spatial encoding, dynamic state correction, and heteroscedastic uncertainty normalization. Because a separate historical model was fitted for each study region, these experiments assess within-site temporal extrapolation across different events rather than transfer of one trained model to unseen geographic regions.

COLD and S-CCD were implemented as baseline estimators following their original harmonic-regression and sequential-update procedures. In this controlled comparison, COLD and S-CCD denote their baseline-estimation components coupled with the same downstream detector used for SINC, rather than their original change-decision rules. For COLD, residual dispersion was represented by the band-wise RMSE of the final stable segment. For S-CCD, predictive standard deviation was derived from the Kalman state covariance and observation variance, subject to the method-specific minimum-RMSE floor; segment RMSE was used when no active near-real-time state was available. For the baseline-extrapolation experiment, COLD, S-CCD, and SINC used the same 80/20 chronological training-holdout split in each study region. For disturbance detection, the three baseline estimators used the same pre-event observation period, target acquisition date, and labeled evaluation pixels. Their multiband residual, CFAR, and SAM responses were processed using the same empirical-null calibration and decision rule described in Section 2.5. Threshold-dependent metrics were calculated using the same labeled pixels, whereas ROC-AUC and PR-AUC were calculated using the intersection of valid labeled samples shared by all three methods.

To avoid favoring SINC during the controlled comparison, the candidate stable support used by all three baseline estimators was determined from the observed reflectance histories rather than from the SINC dynamic branch. Each method then estimated its own empirical-null response distributions and numerical thresholds using the common value of $\alpha$ and the same residual-CFAR-SAM decision rule. Calibration dates were expanded in batches until the relative changes in all three response thresholds were below 2% for two consecutive stages. At least four calibration dates, two validation dates, and 100 expected upper-tail samples were required. This design compares the baseline-estimation components of the three methods under a common downstream detector, rather than their original change-decision rules. Calibration continued until the threshold-stability and tail-support criteria were satisfied or the available candidate history was exhausted. When the history was exhausted before all validation-FAR criteria were met, the final threshold-stable profile was retained and recorded as terminal FAR-limited rather than fully converged. Across the candidate values of $\alpha$, the mean validation FAR was used to select the operating value of $\alpha$, whereas the image-level 95th-percentile FAR was retained as a diagnostic and as the strict profile-convergence criterion.

### 3.4. Validation framework and performance metrics

Because official vector reference layers were unavailable for several events, reference samples were produced by visual interpretation of pre- and post-event Sentinel-2 imagery with support from 0.37-0.41 m WorldView-3 and GeoEye-1 imagery available through Maxar Xpress. Ambiguous boundary pixels were excluded, and approximately balanced sets of clearly interpreted anomaly and normal pixels were initially sampled in each study area. After restriction to the common valid support of all three methods, the retained class counts varied among regions. This design evaluates discrimination between clearly labeled pixels but does not reproduce the operational class prevalence or quantify boundary-label uncertainty; precision, F1-score, and accuracy should therefore be interpreted within this sampling protocol.

Baseline extrapolation accuracy was assessed using root mean square error (RMSE; Eq. (18)) and the structural similarity index (SSIM; Eq. (19)). RMSE was calculated using 1,024 deterministic common-valid pixels for all three methods at each held-out date. In Eq. For each held-out image, eight non-overlapping 16 × 16 patches were selected in a spatially stratified manner from locations at which the observation and the predictions of all three methods were valid, using a fixed random seed of 20260821. Disturbance-detection accuracy was evaluated using Precision (Eq. (20)), Recall (Eq. (21)), F1-score (Eq. (22)), Overall Accuracy (Eq. (23)), and False Alarm Rate (FAR; Eq. (24)). F1-score is the harmonic mean of Precision and Recall and was used as the primary threshold-dependent metric. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC) and the area under the precision-recall curve (PR-AUC) were additionally reported as threshold-independent measures of anomaly-score discrimination (Fawcett, 2006; Saito and Rehmsmeier, 2015). For the paired ablation analysis in Fig. 12B, 95% confidence intervals of the mean event-level F1 difference were estimated by nonparametric bootstrap resampling of the eight study regions with replacement 20,000 times. The interval limits were defined by the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution. Fixed random seeds of 20260816, 20260817, and 20260818 were used for the three ablated configurations, respectively.

Two separate runtime evaluations were conducted. The native-backend comparison for the Landslide scene was performed on a single workstation. SINC was executed on an NVIDIA RTX 4060 Ti GPU because its tiny-cuda-nn hash-grid encoder requires CUDA, whereas the available COLD and S-CCD implementations were executed on the CPU of the same workstation. Each method was repeated five times. Separately, full-scene SINC inference for a 5622 × 5622-pixel image was benchmarked on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin 64 GB developer kit using float16 weights. The Jetson experiment evaluated SINC only as a preliminary assessment of edge-device inference and did not include satellite data input/output, orbital conditions, or a complete power and energy analysis.

## 4. Results

### 4.1. Model size and inference efficiency

Online processing time was first evaluated using the $279 \times 280$-pixel Landslide scene. Each method was run five times, and the results are reported as mean ± standard deviation. The total online time includes loading the stored baseline information, generating the baseline for the target date, and performing empirical-null anomaly detection. Target-image loading and offline model training are not included.

The three methods were run using different processing devices because of their current implementations. The multi-resolution hash-grid encoder in SINC is implemented using tiny-cuda-nn, which requires CUDA. SINC was therefore run on an NVIDIA RTX 4060 Ti GPU. The available implementations of COLD and S-CCD are CPU-based and were run on the CPU of the same workstation. Consequently, the processing times in Table 2 describe the actual runtime of each implementation, but they should not be interpreted as a comparison under identical hardware conditions. Similarly, the online source size denotes the data object required by each evaluated implementation and should not be interpreted as a direct comparison of serialized fitted-model states.

As shown in Table 2, the SINC .gndc file occupied 0.96 MB, whereas the historical image stack used by COLD and S-CCD occupied 123.68 MB. SINC generated the target-date baseline in 0.029 ± 0.037 s and completed the full online procedure in 0.185 ± 0.033 s. The corresponding total online times were 152.143 ± 0.683 s for COLD and 211.855 ± 4.859 s for S-CCD. The empirical-null detection step required a similar amount of time for the three methods. Most of the difference in total processing time therefore occurred during baseline generation. SINC directly obtained the target-date baseline from the stored .gndc file, whereas COLD and S-CCD generated the baseline from the historical image stack.

A separate large-scene experiment was conducted to evaluate the storage requirement and inference efficiency of SINC on the Jetson AGX Orin. For a 5622 × 5622-pixel scene, the two-year Sentinel-2 training image stack occupied 29.4 GB, whereas the trained full-scene SINC deployment state occupied 31.3 MB. The stored baseline was therefore approximately three orders of magnitude smaller than the training archive. The approximately 31.58-million-pixel frame was processed in about 10 s, corresponding to a throughput of approximately 3.1 MP s−1 under the tested implementation. The storage comparison quantifies the reduction from the historical image archive to the learned baseline and does not imply that the original images can be recovered from the .gndc file. The Jetson result represents a SINC-only edge-device benchmark rather than a hardware-matched comparison with COLD and S-CCD.

### 4.2. Baseline extrapolation and spatial reconstruction

Baseline extrapolation was evaluated using a chronological holdout experiment in each of the eight study regions. For each region, the first 80% of the pre-disturbance Sentinel-2 observations were used for model fitting, and the remaining 20% were retained for validation. COLD, S-CCD, and SINC used the same training period and temporal cutoff and predicted the same validation dates. RMSE was calculated over pixels for which the observation and all three predictions were valid. RMSE was evaluated for all 190 held-out images, whereas local-window SSIM was available for 189 images because one Deforestation 1 image did not contain eight common-valid patches.

Fig. 6 summarizes the chronological holdout results across the eight study regions. SINC obtained the lowest regional mean RMSE in the Earthquake region (0.037), compared with 0.070 for COLD and 0.045 for S-CCD. In Flood, the three methods produced similar errors, with mean RMSE values of 0.036, 0.032, and 0.035 for COLD, S-CCD, and SINC, respectively. In the remaining regions, S-CCD generally produced the lowest or nearly lowest mean RMSE. The largest SINC error occurred in Deforestation 1, where its mean RMSE was 0.192, compared with 0.029 for both comparison methods.

When the eight regional means were averaged with equal weight, the mean RMSE values were 0.034 for COLD, 0.028 for S-CCD, and 0.053 for SINC. The corresponding mean local-window SSIM values were 0.875, 0.895, and 0.788. SINC achieved the highest regional SSIM in Earthquake (0.912) and remained close to the comparison methods in Landslide and Deforestation 2, but lower structural similarity was observed in Hurricane (0.654) and Deforestation 1 (0.554). These results show that the compact representation can generate complete target-date baseline images, but its extrapolation accuracy is strongly region dependent and was lower on average than that of COLD and S-CCD.

Fig. 7 presents spatial reconstructions for three illustrative study regions. Across these dates, all three methods recovered the broad land-surface patterns, although differences remained in spectral appearance and local spatial detail. In the Earthquake example, the SINC reconstruction retained the principal terrain structure and was spectrally closer to the held-out observation than the COLD result. In the Vegetation Fire and Wildfire examples, SINC reproduced the main spatial gradients, although some fine-scale texture was smoothed. These examples provide qualitative illustrations only; quantitative results across all 190 held-out images are reported in Fig. 6.

### 4.3. Empirical-null calibration of anomaly thresholds

To avoid selecting anomaly thresholds from the eight disturbance cases, the operating false-alarm level was calibrated using only normal historical observations, following the procedure described in Section 2.5. For each study region, the accepted normal observations were divided into calibration and validation subsets. The calibration subset was used to estimate the 95th-percentile reference scales of the multiband squared standardized residual, CFAR, and SAM responses, and the $(1 - \alpha)$-quantile threshold of the resulting normalized joint score. The held-out normal validation subset was subsequently used to evaluate the realized false-alarm rate (FAR).

The candidate tail probabilities were $\alpha \in \ \left\{ 0.01,\ 0.005,\ 0.001,\ 0.0005,\ 0.0001,\ 0.00005,\ 0.00001 \right\}$. Following the principles of statistical detection and constant false-alarm rate control, the candidate range was restricted to $\alpha \leq 0.01$ , ensuring that the joint-score threshold was estimated from at least the 99th percentile of its empirical null distribution (Manolakis and Shaw, 2002; Reed and Yu, 1990). A related probability level has been used in COLD, where a change probability of 0.99 corresponds to an upper-tail probability of 0.01, although COLD additionally requires consecutive anomalous observations to confirm a change (Zhu et al., 2020). In the present study, 0.01 therefore represents the least conservative candidate false-alarm level rather than a universal optimal threshold.

As shown in Fig. 8A, decreasing $\alpha$ progressively reduced the false-alarm rate on the held-out normal observations. At $\alpha = 0.01$, the mean FAR across the 238 validation images was 0.0094, which remained below the predefined target of 0.01. Although smaller candidate values also satisfied this criterion, they produced more conservative thresholds. Therefore, $\alpha = 0.01$ was selected as the largest candidate value whose mean normal FAR did not exceed the target. This selection did not use disturbance labels or detection accuracy for the target events. The 95th percentile of the image-level FAR was 0.042, indicating that the criterion controls the average FAR rather than imposing the same FAR on every acquisition.

After fixing $\alpha = 0.01$, the empirical-null procedure produced different numerical thresholds for the eight study regions (Fig. 8B). The effective $\chi^{2}$, CFAR, and SAM thresholds ranged from 3.37 to 31.84, from 1.37 to 12.16, and from 0.237 to 1.347, respectively. These differences reflect region-specific normal variability in spectral residual magnitude, local spatial contrast, and spectral direction. Thus, all regions use the same false-alarm calibration rule, while their numerical thresholds are determined separately from their respective normal historical observations.

Strict profile convergence was reached for the Vegetation Fire region. For the remaining seven regions, the threshold-stability and tail-sample-support requirements were satisfied, but at least one baseline estimator did not meet the strict image-level FAR criterion before the available candidate history was exhausted. Their final threshold-stable profiles were therefore retained according to the prespecified terminal rule in Section 3.3.

### 4.4. Disturbance detection performance

Fig. 9 compares the disturbance-detection performance of SINC, COLD, and S-CCD across the eight study regions. All three methods were evaluated using thresholds calibrated from normal historical observations at $\alpha = 0.01$, as described in Section 4.3. These thresholds were fixed before the target disturbance images were evaluated and were not selected by maximizing the F1-score of each event. Fig. 9A reports the threshold-dependent metrics, Fig. 9B reports ROC-AUC and PR-AUC, and Fig. 9C summarizes the unweighted macro-average across the eight regions. The complete region-level values are provided in Table S1.

Across the eight regions, SINC achieved macro-averaged Accuracy, Recall, Precision, and F1-score values of 80.87%, 67.15%, 87.93%, and 73.39%, respectively. The corresponding F1-scores were 59.18% for COLD and 55.80% for S-CCD. SINC therefore improved the macro-averaged F1-score by 14.21 and 17.59 percentage points relative to COLD and S-CCD, respectively. Its FAR was 7.75%, compared with 10.57% for COLD and 8.83% for S-CCD.

Performance varied among disturbance types. SINC obtained the highest F1-score in six of the eight regions, including Flood, Earthquake, Landslide, Hurricane, Deforestation 1, and Deforestation 2. The clearest differences occurred for earthquake, where the F1-scores were 77.01%, 16.07%, and 14.81% for SINC, COLD, and S-CCD, respectively, and for Landslide, where the corresponding values were 94.12%, 81.67%, and 83.87%. COLD performed best for Wildfire, whereas S-CCD obtained the highest F1-score for Vegetation Fire. These results show that the relative performance remained disturbance dependent.

The macro-averaged ROC-AUC and PR-AUC values of SINC were 91.08% and 89.38%, respectively. The corresponding values were 83.50% and 80.30% for COLD, and 81.40% and 80.44% for S-CCD. These results indicate that SINC achieved the highest macro-averaged class-separation performance before binary thresholding. Because a separate baseline was trained and calibrated for each region, the experiment evaluates within-region temporal monitoring rather than geographic transfer of a single trained model.

The pixel-based metrics in Fig. 9 summarize detection performance but do not show the spatial distribution of the detected anomalies. Fig. 10 therefore provides a detailed comparison for the Landslide region, including full-scene anomaly masks, local enlargements of a mapped landslide, and SWIR2 reflectance trajectories for a representative pixel. Fig. 11 extends the spatial comparison to all eight study regions.

In the Landslide example, all three methods located the main disturbed area but produced different spatial coverage and boundary patterns (Fig. 10A–H). At the labeled evaluation samples, SINC obtained a Recall of 91.43%, Precision of 96.97%, and F1-score of 94.12%. The corresponding F1-scores were 81.67% for COLD and 83.87% for S-CCD. The local enlargements illustrate the differences in omission and spatial coverage under the calibrated thresholds. The SWIR2 trajectories in Fig. 10I provide a temporal view of the same disturbance. The target observation on 28 May 2024 showed a clear positive deviation from the predicted mean and lay outside the displayed $\pm 2\sigma$ range for all three methods. The fitted trajectories and uncertainty ranges differed because COLD, S-CCD, and SINC used different baseline representations. For SINC, the uncertainty range was obtained from the predicted heteroscedastic dispersion. This panel illustrates how the target observation was evaluated at one representative pixel and does not provide an independent assessment of uncertainty calibration. Because the three methods estimate residual dispersion differently, the shaded ranges are interpreted within each method and are not treated as directly equivalent calibrated probability intervals across methods.

Fig. 11 shows that the spatial differences among the methods varied across the eight study regions. In Earthquake and Hurricane, COLD and S-CCD detected relatively little of the mapped disturbance, whereas SINC retained a larger response, consistent with the Recall results in Fig. 9. SINC also obtained the highest F1-score in Flood, Landslide, Deforestation 1, and Deforestation 2. The visual comparisons therefore do not independently establish boundary accuracy or object-level detection performance.

### 4.6. Ablation analysis

To examine the contributions of the main SINC components, three ablated configurations were evaluated across the eight study regions: removal of the multi-resolution hash-grid encoder, removal of the dynamic state correction branch, and removal of heteroscedastic uncertainty modeling. Each configuration was calibrated independently using normal historical observations, following the same empirical-null procedure with $\alpha = 0.01$. The disturbance observations were not used for threshold selection. Table 3 summarizes the macro-averaged performance and model size, while Fig. 12 presents the event-level F1-scores and the paired changes relative to the complete model.

The complete SINC model achieved macro-averaged Accuracy, Recall, Precision, FAR, and F1-score values of 80.87%, 67.15%, 87.93%, 7.75%, and 73.39%, respectively. Removing dynamic correction produced the largest reduction: Recall decreased to 8.61% and the F1-score decreased to 12.15%. The mean event-level F1 loss was 0.612, and the complete model performed better in all eight regions. Because the model size changed only from 0.958 to 0.953 MB, this component improved detection performance without materially increasing storage.

Removing hash-grid encoding reduced the model size to 0.063 MB, but also decreased Recall to 28.90% and the F1-score to 38.56%. The mean event-level F1 loss was 0.348, with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval of 0.198–0.508. The complete model performed better in all eight regions, indicating that the hash-grid encoder contributes to the spatially varying representation of baseline parameters.

Removing heteroscedasticity reduced the macro-averaged F1-score from 73.39% to 66.00%. The mean event-level F1 loss was 0.074, with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval of 0.031–0.129, and the complete model performed better in seven of the eight regions. Its contribution was therefore smaller than those of hash-grid encoding and dynamic correction, but remained positive under the current empirical-null calibration.

The spatial examples in Fig. 13 are consistent with the quantitative results. Removing the dynamic state correction branch resulted in substantial omission in the Wildfire and Deforestation 2 regions, while removing the hash-grid encoder also reduced the detected disturbance extent. The results without heteroscedasticity were generally closer to those of the complete model, but differences in background detections remained region dependent. These spatial patterns are consistent with the quantitative component effects summarized in Fig. 12 and Table 3.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1. Interpretation of the spatially continuous baseline

The main contribution of SINC is the compact representation of the normal reflectance baseline, while disturbance detection serves as a downstream evaluation of this representation. Instead of storing an independent set of harmonic coefficients for every pixel, SINC uses shared neural-network parameters to generate spatially varying harmonic coefficients and residual variance. For the 5622 × 5622-pixel scene, this representation reduced the stored baseline state from a 29.4-GB historical image stack to a .gndc deployment state of 31.3 MB. This reduction reflects the storage efficiency of the learned baseline representation and does not imply lossless compression or recovery of the original observations.

The chronological holdout experiment revealed a trade-off between compact storage and extrapolation accuracy. When the eight regional means were weighted equally, SINC obtained a mean RMSE of 0.053 and a mean local-window SSIM of 0.788, compared with 0.034 and 0.875 for COLD and 0.028 and 0.895 for S-CCD, respectively. SINC performed best in the Earthquake region but showed larger errors in Hurricane and especially Deforestation 1. Thus, the shared implicit representation provides compact storage and complete spatial queries, but it does not guarantee uniformly accurate temporal extrapolation across regions. The regional differences indicate that the effectiveness of the shared parameterization depends on the temporal behavior of each region.

The ablation results clarify the roles of the principal components. Removing hash-grid encoding reduced the model size from 0.958 to 0.063 MB, but decreased the macro-averaged F1-score from 73.39% to 38.56%. The complete model performed better in all eight regions, indicating that hash-grid encoding contributes to the spatially varying representation of baseline parameters rather than merely increasing model size. Removing dynamic correction produced the largest performance reduction. Although the model size remained nearly unchanged at 0.953 MB, Recall decreased to 8.61% and the F1-score decreased to 12.15%. This result supports the role of dynamic correction in representing sustained baseline-level changes that are not fully described by the harmonic component alone.

Heteroscedastic uncertainty modeling produced a smaller but positive contribution. Removing it reduced the macro-averaged F1-score from 73.39% to 66.00%, and the complete model performed better in seven of the eight regions. The mean paired F1 loss was 0.074, with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval of 0.031–0.129. Because each configuration was calibrated independently using normal historical observations, the empirical-null thresholds may partly compensate for changes in residual scale. The result should therefore be interpreted as the contribution of heteroscedastic uncertainty under the common downstream calibration procedure, rather than as direct evidence that the predicted uncertainty is fully calibrated. Independent coverage analyses remain necessary.

### 5.2. Empirical-null calibration and deployment implications

The empirical-null procedure reduces dependence on disturbance labels during threshold selection. A common tail probability of $\alpha = 0.01$ was selected using only normal calibration and validation observations. At this operating point, the mean FAR over the 238 held-out normal validation images was 0.0094. The resulting numerical thresholds nevertheless differed among regions because the normal residual, CFAR, and SAM distributions were estimated separately for each baseline. Therefore, $\alpha = 0.01$ defines a common calibration rule rather than a universal numerical anomaly threshold. The image-level 95th-percentile FAR of 0.0420 also indicates that satisfying the mean FAR constraint does not guarantee the same false-alarm rate for every acquisition.

Using these independently calibrated regional thresholds, SINC achieved a macro-averaged F1-score of 73.39%, compared with 59.18% for COLD and 55.80% for S-CCD. SINC obtained the highest event-level F1-score in six of the eight regions, S-CCD performed best for Vegetation Fire, and COLD performed best for Wildfire. SINC also achieved macro-averaged ROC-AUC and PR-AUC values of 91.08% and 89.38%, respectively. These results show that the learned baseline can support disturbance detection across different environmental settings, but the relative performance remains disturbance dependent. Because a separate baseline was trained and calibrated for each region, the experiment evaluates within-region temporal monitoring and does not demonstrate geographic transfer of a single trained model.

The efficiency results indicate potential for resource-constrained deployment. In the Landslide scene, SINC generated the target-date baseline directly from the stored .gndc state, whereas COLD and S-CCD processed the historical image stack. The measured runtime reflects both algorithmic and hardware differences because SINC used an RTX 4060 Ti GPU and the available COLD and S-CCD implementations used the CPU of the same workstation. In the separate Jetson AGX Orin experiment, SINC processed the approximately 31.58-million-pixel scene in about 10 s, corresponding to approximately 3.1 MP $s^{-1}$. This result supports further investigation of edge-device inference but does not constitute a hardware-matched comparison or demonstrate operational on-orbit processing.

Building on this edge-device evaluation, an engineering implementation of SINC has been integrated into the onboard processing system of Beijing Normal University’s Muduo-1A/1B instant remote-sensing experimental satellite constellation, which was successfully launched into orbit on 25 August 2026 (China National Space Administration, 2026). The flight implementation retains the same spatiotemporal baseline learning and uncertainty-normalized anomaly scoring evaluated in this study. To support deterministic and low-latency onboard inference, however, it directly applies a preset numerical decision threshold rather than estimating an empirical-null distribution onboard. Thus, the experimental and flight implementations share the same representation and scoring backbone but adopt different thresholding configurations. The present study does not report in-orbit performance, which will be evaluated during subsequent commissioning and operational tests.

### 5.3. Limitations and future work

Several limitations define the scope of the present conclusions. First, a separate SINC model was trained for each study region. The experiments therefore assess temporal extrapolation and disturbance detection within previously observed regions rather than geographic transfer to unseen areas. Although the empirical-null thresholds were calibrated without using disturbance labels, the common value of $\alpha$ was evaluated using normal observations from the same eight regions. Independent geographic sites and longer stable time series are needed to determine whether this operating rule transfers across sensors, climates, and land-cover conditions.

Second, the reference samples were manually selected to represent clear anomaly and normal conditions, and ambiguous boundary pixels were excluded. After common-valid filtering, the class proportions varied among regions. This design supports controlled pixel-level comparison but does not reproduce operational anomaly prevalence or quantify boundary uncertainty. Future evaluation should include spatially complete reference polygons, object-level metrics, naturally imbalanced samples, and independent event datasets. The predicted variance varies with spatial location, spectral band, normalized time, and extrapolation distance. However, its conditional Gaussian assumption and empirical coverage have not yet been evaluated independently. Coverage analyses of the predicted $\pm \sigma$ and $\pm 2\sigma$ intervals are therefore needed before the uncertainty output is interpreted as a calibrated predictive interval (Kuleshov et al., 2018).

Finally, the present evaluation is limited to binary disturbance detection from Sentinel-2 optical observations and does not identify disturbance type. Persistent cloud cover may also limit baseline estimation and monitoring, while integration with SAR could improve temporal coverage. Although an engineering implementation of SINC has been integrated into the Muduo-1 constellation, the experiments reported here remain ground based and do not evaluate satellite data ingestion, radiation effects, power consumption, orbital latency, or long-term operational stability. These factors will be examined during subsequent in-orbit commissioning and validation.

## 6. Conclusions

This study introduced SINC as a compact and continuous representation of historical reflectance baselines in satellite time series. A shared implicit neural field generates spatially varying harmonic coefficients and heteroscedastic uncertainty, while dynamic correction captures sustained baseline shifts, thereby retaining explicit seasonal structure without independently fitting and storing parameters for every pixel. After training on a 29.4-GB two-year Sentinel-2 image stack, SINC represented the learned full-scene baseline using a 31.3-MB deployment state and processed a 31.58-million-pixel scene in approximately 10 s on the Jetson AGX Orin. As a downstream disturbance-detection application, SINC achieved a macro-averaged F1-score of 73.39%, compared with 59.18% for COLD and 55.80% for S-CCD, and obtained the highest event-level F1-score in six of the eight study regions, although it did not outperform the comparison methods in the vegetation-fire and wildfire cases. Ablation experiments showed that hash-grid encoding and dynamic correction made the largest contributions, while uncertainty normalization provided a smaller but positive contribution to detection performance under the current calibration. An engineering implementation retaining the same baseline representation and uncertainty-normalized scoring, but using a preset onboard decision threshold, has been integrated into the Muduo-1 constellation for subsequent in-orbit validation. The present evidence remains limited to separately fitted regional models, region-specific empirical-null calibration, manually interpreted pixel samples, and ground-based performance evaluation. Within these bounds, SINC provides a promising framework for compact, uncertainty-aware baseline modeling and continuous land-surface disturbance monitoring under resource-constrained conditions.
